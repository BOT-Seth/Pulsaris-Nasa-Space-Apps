## Project Name
Pulsaris

## High-Level Project Summary
Pulsaris is an offline health self-assessment app for astronauts. In one screen it tells a crew member what needs attention, why, and what to do next. A 30-second check-in (mood, sleepiness) joins sleep and heart data and entered readings (blood pressure, blood sugar, temperature). Explicit rules flag each indicator as no flag, Watch or Act; when fatigue signs appear, a 3-minute reaction-time test checks alertness. The app then suggests a concrete countermeasure, records what the crew member did and schedules a re-check. This matters because on Mars-class missions medical advice from Earth can lag up to 24 minutes each way, and sleep loss, the most common risk, is quiet: ISS astronauts in a NASA-funded study averaged about 6 hours of sleep against 8.5 scheduled. Pulsaris turns readings into a decision the crew can act on without a network.

## Detailed Project Description
**What it does.** Pulsaris covers all six indicators the challenge names (blood pressure, heart rate, blood sugar, body temperature, sleep hours, mood) plus heart rate variability, sleepiness (Karolinska Sleepiness Scale) and a 3-minute reaction-time test. The Today screen shows one status (no flag, Watch or Act), the reason, the readings behind it and one next step. Measurements shows every indicator color-coded with its trend, its rule and its source. A 3D body map groups readings by body system and is where symptoms are reported. Plan & log keeps a timestamped record and JSON export.

**How it works.** Each indicator has an explicit, visible rule (for example: Watch when sleep is under 5 h; Watch when the reaction test is at least 1 SD slower than the comparison, Act at 1.5 SD or 6+ lapses). Heart data are compared with the crew member's own in-flight baseline, because heart rate and blood pressure change in microgravity. The reaction test follows the 3-minute PVT design NASA uses on the ISS (Reaction Self Test) and is compared on a scale informed by ISS astronaut data from NASA's Human Research Program (Dev et al., 2024). Recording an action never clears a concern; only a re-check does. A crew member can mark a record for medical review.

**Trust and honesty.** Every value is tagged with where it came from: Earth recording, generated example, crew entry or device measurement. Flags come from deterministic rules, never from AI. The app runs fully offline from a single HTML file, on tablet and phone, and no data leaves the device. It is a prototype: not a medical device, not tested with astronauts, and its thresholds are labeled "not clinically validated."

**Benefits and goals.** Less crew time spent interpreting numbers; earlier, consistent attention to fatigue; and a record a flight surgeon can review when contact returns. Next steps: review the rules with a flight surgeon, run a supervised usability study in a simulated mission, validate device timing and thresholds, and connect live wearables (wrist actigraphy as used in NASA's ARCHeR study on Artemis II; CSA Bio-Monitor).

**Tools.** React 19, TypeScript, Vite, Three.js (3D body), Vitest (17 automated tests on the scoring and rules), Python and Node scripts for data preparation. Hosted on Vercel; also builds to one self-contained offline HTML file.

## NASA Data
- NASA Human Research Program Standard Measures: Dev S. et al., Frontiers in Physiology, 2024 (pre-flight ISS astronaut PVT speed 5.25 ± 0.24, N = 24) — sets the comparison scale for the alertness test. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269
- NASA Reaction Self Test (3-minute PVT used on the ISS since 2009) — test design. https://spinoff.nasa.gov/Spinoff2013/hm_1.html
- NASA HRP Risk from Inadequate Sleep and Irregular Schedules; NASA HRP 5 Hazards of Human Spaceflight — "Why it matters in space" text and countermeasure wording. https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-from-inadequate-sleep-and-irregular-schedules/ · https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/
- NASA Earth-Independent Medical Operations Concept of Operations (2024) — design target for on-board decisions with delayed ground support. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001402
- Fritsch-Yelle J.M. et al., J. Applied Physiology 80(3), 1996 (NASA JSC) — heart data compared with in-flight nights. https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1996.80.3.910
- NASA ARCHeR (Artemis II crew health and readiness) — wrist actigraphy as a target data source. https://www.nasa.gov/reference/archer
